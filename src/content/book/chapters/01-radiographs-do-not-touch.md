Most bone lesions you will see as a resident are found by accident: a knee radiograph for a twisted knee, an ankle series after a fall, a chest film that happens to include the humerus. The patient did not come for the lesion. Your job is not to name every lesion in the textbook. Your job is to do two things well, every single time.

> **Task 1. Can I name it and leave it alone?** Is this a classic benign lesion, a "do not touch" lesion, that needs no biopsy and usually no follow-up?
>
> **Task 2. If not, how aggressive is it, and how fast must the patient move?** Score it, recommend the right next step, and send the patient to the right person before anyone puts a needle in it.

A "do not touch" lesion is one whose radiographic appearance is so characteristic that biopsy would add risk and nothing else. Biopsy of some of these lesions is not just unnecessary: myositis ossificans and a healing stress fracture can look like sarcoma under the microscope, and a needle can turn a benign finding into a surgical case. The phrase is old teaching folklore in skeletal radiology; this chapter gives it a structure by mapping it to the American College of Radiology Bone Reporting and Data System (Bone-RADS).

This chapter is written for the first-year resident and has two parts:

- **Part A. Bone lesions.** The features that separate benign from malignant, how to score them, the classic benign lesions you will see every week (non-ossifying fibroma, enchondroma, bone island and others), and the classic malignant tumors you must recognize (osteosarcoma, chondrosarcoma, Ewing sarcoma, lymphoma).
- **Part B. Soft-tissue masses.** The same benign-versus-malignant question for a lump in the soft tissues, and what radiographs, ultrasound and MRI each add.

# Part A. Bone lesions

## 1.1 The core concept: benign or malignant?

Start with one comparison. A benign bone lesion grows slowly, so the bone has time to wall it off. A malignant lesion grows fast, so the bone cannot keep up and the tumor breaks out.

| Feature | Benign ("do not touch") | Malignant (aggressive) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Margin** | Well circumscribed, narrow zone of transition, often a thin sclerotic rim | Ill-defined, wide zone of transition, moth-eaten or permeative |
| **Endosteal scalloping** | None or mild | Deep (more than two thirds of the cortex) |
| **Periosteal reaction** | None, or solid and smooth | Interrupted: lamellated, spiculated, Codman triangle |
| **Cortex** | Intact | Destroyed, with breakthrough |
| **Beyond the bone** | Nothing | Soft-tissue mass |

If a lesion has every feature in the left column, it is very likely benign. Any feature from the right column moves it toward Task 2. The rest of Part A puts numbers and names on this table.

## 1.2 Reading a lesion on the radiograph

Before you sort a lesion, describe it the same way every time. The radiograph is still the best test for how fast a bone lesion is growing, because the bone's response (the margin, the periosteum, the cortex) is a record of the lesion's speed (Miller 2008).

1. **Patient age.** Under 30 and over 40 are different worlds. In a patient over 40, a solitary lytic lesion is metastasis or myeloma until proven otherwise.
2. **Bone and location.** Epiphysis, metaphysis or diaphysis; central, eccentric, cortical or surface. Many benign lesions have a favorite address (non-ossifying fibroma: eccentric metaphysis of the distal femur or tibia; chondroblastoma: epiphysis of a young patient).
3. **Margin and zone of transition.** The single most important feature. A narrow zone of transition means slow growth; a wide or invisible one means fast growth.
4. **Periosteal reaction.** Solid and smooth means slow; interrupted, layered, spiculated or a Codman triangle means the periosteum could not keep up.
5. **Cortex.** Endosteal scalloping (how deep?), expansion, breakthrough.
6. **Matrix.** Chondroid ("rings and arcs", popcorn), osteoid (cloud-like, fluffy), ground glass (fibrous dysplasia), or none.
7. **Soft-tissue mass.** Look for displaced fat planes.
8. **Number.** One lesion or many.
9. **Symptoms.** Pain at rest or at night, a growing lump, and pain that is clearly coming from the lesion all move a lesion toward Task 2.

### Margins: the Lodwick–Madewell grades

ACR Bone-RADS uses a modified Lodwick–Madewell grading of the lesion margin (Caracciolo et al. 2023):

| Grade | Margin | What it means |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **IA** | Geographic, well defined, with a sclerotic rim | Very slow. The bone had time to wall it off. |
| **IB** | Geographic, well defined, no sclerotic rim | Slow, but not slow enough to build a rim. |
| **II** | Geographic but ill-defined (wide zone of transition) | Faster. Benign lesions can do this, but many malignant ones do. |
| **IIIA** | Changing margins (a geographic lesion that has become ill-defined) | Something new is happening. |
| **IIIB** | Non-geographic: moth-eaten or permeative | Fast. Think round cell tumor, infection, myeloma, metastasis. |
| **IIIC** | Radiographically occult; seen only on MRI, CT or bone scan | Marrow replaced before the trabeculae are destroyed. |

## 1.3 Bone-RADS: turning the two tasks into a score

In 2023 the ACR Bone-RADS Committee, with the Musculoskeletal Tumor Society, published a radiograph-based risk stratification and management system for any newly found, potentially neoplastic bone lesion (Caracciolo et al. 2023). You add points for six features and the total gives the category.

| Feature | Points |
| --- | --- |
| **Margin** | IA = 1 · IB = 3 · II = 5 · IIIA–C = 7 |
| **Periosteal reaction** | None = 0 · Non-aggressive = 2 · Aggressive = 4 |
| **Endosteal erosion** | Mild (< 1/3 cortex) = 0 · Moderate (1/3–2/3) = 1 · Deep (> 2/3, or cortical breach) = 2 |
| **Pathologic fracture** | Yes = 2 |
| **Soft-tissue mass** | Yes = 4 |
| **Known primary cancer** | Yes = 2 |

| Category | Points | Meaning | Management (ACR) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Bone-RADS 0** | n/a | Incompletely characterized | More views or cross-sectional imaging first. Example: a lucent lesion of the pelvis, scapula or spine. |
| **Bone-RADS 1** | 1–2 | Very low risk. Pathognomonic benign lesion, the classic "do not touch" lesion | If asymptomatic, workup can be complete, or annual surveillance to confirm stability. If symptomatic, advanced imaging and orthopedic oncology referral to treat the benign tumor. |
| **Bone-RADS 2** | 3–4 | Low risk. Asymptomatic geographic lytic lesion without suspicious periosteal reaction or deep endosteal erosion | Short-interval surveillance (3–6 months); consider advanced imaging, biopsy only if needed to confirm benignity. |
| **Bone-RADS 3** | 5–6 | Intermediate risk | Advanced imaging (CT, MRI or bone scan) and orthopedic oncology referral for probable biopsy. |
| **Bone-RADS 4** | ≥ 7 | High risk. Malignant until proven otherwise | Orthopedic oncology referral for biopsy and treatment planning; staging. |

Notice how the arithmetic is built: **a sclerotic rim alone gives 1 point, and any margin of grade II or worse with no other finding already puts the lesion in Bone-RADS 3 or 4**. The margin carries the system, exactly as it carries your eye.

### Worked examples

- **Non-ossifying fibroma** in a 14-year-old: IA margin (1), no periosteal reaction (0), mild endosteal thinning (0). Total 1: **Bone-RADS 1**. Name it and leave it.
- **Enchondroma** of the proximal humerus in a 52-year-old, found on a shoulder series: IB margin (3), no periosteal reaction (0), moderate endosteal scalloping (1). Total 4: **Bone-RADS 2**. Probably benign; confirm stability.
- **The same patient has breast cancer**: 3 + 0 + 1 + 2 = 6: **Bone-RADS 3**. The known primary changes the pretest probability, and the score reflects it.
- **Moth-eaten destruction** of the femoral diaphysis with lamellated periosteal reaction: 7 + 4 = 11: **Bone-RADS 4**, even before you look for the soft-tissue mass.

**Caveats you must know.** The point values are expert consensus and, in the authors' own words, would benefit from validation. The score does not replace pattern recognition: some lesions that can be named with certainty (a bone island, a fibrous dysplasia with ground glass matrix) are recognized by their pattern, not by their points. Bone-RADS 1 does not mean "never treated": the committee lists **osteoid osteoma** as Bone-RADS 1 because it is pathognomonic, but a painful osteoid osteoma is treated, usually by ablation. *Do not touch* means *do not biopsy and do not work up*, not *do not help the patient*.

A different document, the Society of Skeletal Radiology Bone-RADS white paper (Chang et al. 2022), covers **incidental solitary bone lesions on CT and MRI in adults**. You will meet it in the tumor chapter; the logic (benign, follow, biopsy) is the same.

## 1.4 The classic benign lesions: the "do not touch" gallery

The three you will see most often come first: non-ossifying fibroma, enchondroma and bone island. For each lesion: what you see, why you can leave it alone, and what would make you touch it. The red flags are the bridge to Task 2.

<div class="lesion">

### Non-ossifying fibroma and fibrous cortical defect

<div class="ex-slot" data-topic="nof"></div>

**Who and where.** Children and adolescents. Eccentric, metaphyseal, long bones of the lower limb (distal femur, proximal and distal tibia). A small lesion confined to the cortex is a fibrous cortical defect; a larger one extending into the medulla is a non-ossifying fibroma (NOF). They are the same histology (metaphyseal fibrous defect).

**What you see.** A lucent, eccentric, often multilocular-looking ("bubbly") lesion with a thin sclerotic rim (IA) and a scalloped sclerotic border toward the medulla. The long axis parallels the bone. As the child grows the lesion migrates away from the physis and fills in with sclerosis, which can look odd but is the normal healing course.

**Why you can leave it.** Pathognomonic appearance, and the ACR's own example of a Bone-RADS 1 lesion.

**When to touch it.** Pain or a pathologic fracture. Large lesions occupying most of the bone width carry fracture risk in weight-bearing bones and deserve an orthopedic opinion. Multiple NOFs with café-au-lait spots suggest neurofibromatosis type 1 or Jaffe–Campanacci syndrome.

**Report as.** "Non-ossifying fibroma. Benign; no further imaging is needed."

</div>

<div class="lesion">

### Enchondroma

<div class="ex-slot" data-topic="enchondroma"></div>

**What you see.** A central, metaphyseal or diaphyseal lesion with **chondroid matrix: rings and arcs, popcorn or punctate calcification**. In the hands it is the commonest bone tumor, usually lucent, expansile and often with thin cortex. In long bones (proximal humerus, distal femur) it is usually found by accident.

**Why you can leave it.** Small, asymptomatic, with mild endosteal scalloping and no cortical change. These are often Bone-RADS 2, not 1, because the margin may lack a sclerotic rim: they get follow-up, not biopsy.

**When to touch it.** This is the most important borderline in the chapter, because the next diagnosis is low-grade chondrosarcoma, and the two can be indistinguishable under the microscope. Features that favor chondrosarcoma in long bones (Murphey et al. 1998): **deep endosteal scalloping (more than two thirds of the cortical thickness)**, scalloping along a long segment, cortical destruction or thickening, periosteal reaction, a soft-tissue mass, a large lesion (long-bone chondrosarcomas are typically larger than 5 cm), and **pain that is coming from the lesion** rather than from the joint. In the hand, aggressive-looking enchondromas are usually still benign; in the pelvis, ribs and shoulder girdle, treat any cartilage lesion with suspicion.

**Do not forget.** An enchondroma and a bone infarct can look alike. Chondroid calcification is central, in rings and arcs; an infarct has a **serpiginous sclerotic border** and no endosteal scalloping.

**Report as.** "Chondroid lesion, most consistent with enchondroma. No features of aggressive behavior." Then give the follow-up you recommend.

</div>

<div class="lesion">

### Bone island (enostosis)

<div class="ex-slot" data-topic="bone-island"></div>

**What you see.** A dense, homogeneous focus of compact bone in the medulla, round or oval, usually under 2 cm, with **brush-like, spiculated margins (thorny radiations)** that blend with the surrounding trabeculae. Common in the pelvis, proximal femur and spine.

**Why you can leave it.** Bone islands are normal cortical bone in the wrong place. The brush border is the key: it shows the lesion is integrated with the trabeculae, unlike the smoother margin of many blastic metastases.

**When to touch it.** A known primary cancer (prostate, breast) with a new sclerotic lesion, a lesion that grows quickly, a size over 2 cm (a "giant" bone island is still usually benign, but needs care), or pain. Bone islands are usually not avid on bone scan; marked uptake should make you think again. On CT, attenuation helps: in untreated patients, a mean below **885 HU** or a maximum below **1060 HU** favors metastasis over bone island (sensitivity and specificity about 95% each; Ulano et al. 2016).

**Report as.** "Bone island (enostosis). Benign."

</div>

<div class="lesion">

### Osteochondroma

<div class="ex-slot" data-topic="osteochondroma"></div>

**What you see.** A bony outgrowth, sessile or pedunculated, from the metaphysis, pointing away from the joint. The diagnostic sign: **the cortex of the lesion is continuous with the cortex of the bone, and the marrow of the lesion is continuous with the marrow of the bone.** No other lesion does this.

**Why you can leave it.** Continuity is pathognomonic. Asymptomatic osteochondromas need nothing.

**When to touch it.** New pain or growth after skeletal maturity, irregular or destroyed mineralization in the cap, or a soft-tissue mass. A **cartilage cap thicker than about 2 cm in an adult**, measured on MRI or CT, raises concern for secondary chondrosarcoma (Bernard et al. 2010). Multiple lesions (hereditary multiple exostoses) carry a higher lifetime risk of malignant change. Benign complications that do need attention: fracture of the stalk, bursa formation over the cap, and compression of vessels or nerves (popliteal pseudoaneurysm, peroneal nerve).

**Report as.** "Osteochondroma, with cortical and medullary continuity." If MRI is done, report the cap thickness.

</div>

<div class="lesion">

### Simple (unicameral) bone cyst

<div class="ex-slot" data-topic="simple-bone-cyst"></div>

**What you see.** A central, lucent, mildly expansile metaphyseal lesion in a child, classically in the proximal humerus or proximal femur, with a thin sclerotic rim and the long axis along the bone. After a fracture, a piece of cortex can fall to the dependent part of the fluid-filled cyst: the **fallen fragment sign**, which proves the lesion is hollow.

**Why you can leave it.** Classic site and appearance. A simple bone cyst is not a tumor that needs a biopsy.

**When to touch it.** Fracture risk drives treatment (proximal femur especially). If the lesion is eccentric, markedly expansile, or has fluid levels on MRI, think aneurysmal bone cyst instead, which is a different conversation.

</div>

<div class="lesion">

### Fibrous dysplasia

<div class="ex-slot" data-topic="fibrous-dysplasia"></div>

**What you see.** A long, central, intramedullary lesion of the diaphysis or metaphysis with a **ground-glass** matrix (hazier than lytic bone, less dense than sclerotic bone), often with a thick sclerotic rind, mild expansion and endosteal scalloping. Bowing deformity of the proximal femur gives the **shepherd's crook**. Ribs, femur, tibia, skull base and face are common sites (Kransdorf et al. 1990).

**Why you can leave it.** The ground-glass matrix in the right setting is diagnostic.

**When to touch it.** Pain, fracture, progressive deformity, or a new lytic or destructive area (rare malignant transformation). Polyostotic disease with endocrine abnormalities and café-au-lait spots is McCune–Albright syndrome; with intramuscular myxomas it is Mazabraud syndrome.

</div>

<div class="lesion">

### Intraosseous lipoma

<div class="ex-slot" data-topic="intraosseous-lipoma"></div>

**What you see.** A well-defined lucent lesion, classically in the calcaneus between the trabecular groups of the neck (the same triangle where the normal calcaneal pseudocyst lives), often with a **central calcification** from fat necrosis. Also the proximal femur and other metaphyses.

**Why you can leave it.** Fat attenuation on CT or fat signal on MRI confirms it. Fat is a reassuring finding in bone (Chapter 2).

**Do not forget.** A calcaneal lucency without central calcification may be a normal pseudocyst (sparse trabeculae), a lipoma, or a simple bone cyst. All three are usually left alone.

</div>

<div class="lesion">

### Bone infarct

<div class="ex-slot" data-topic="bone-infarct"></div>

**What you see.** A medullary, metadiaphyseal lesion with a **serpiginous, sclerotic, shell-like border**, often in both knees or both shoulders, in patients with steroid use, sickle cell disease, alcohol use or prior chemotherapy. No endosteal scalloping, no cortical change.

**When to touch it.** Rarely, sarcoma (undifferentiated pleomorphic sarcoma) arises in an old infarct: a new lytic area, cortical destruction or a soft-tissue mass changes the category.

</div>

<div class="lesion">

### Myositis ossificans

<div class="ex-slot" data-topic="myositis-ossificans"></div>

**What you see.** Soft-tissue ossification near, but separate from, a bone, often after trauma that the patient may not remember. Maturation follows a **zonal pattern: mature, well-formed bone at the periphery and immature tissue in the center**, the reverse of a surface osteosarcoma, which is densest at its center. A thin lucent cleft often separates it from the cortex.

**Why you must not biopsy it.** Early myositis ossificans is cellular and mitotically active and can be mistaken for sarcoma on a biopsy. If the picture is not yet mature, the right next step is **short-term follow-up radiographs or CT** to watch the peripheral shell form, not a needle.

</div>

<div class="lesion">

### Healing stress fracture and avulsion injuries

<div class="ex-slot" data-topic="stress-fracture"></div>

**What you see.** Solid periosteal reaction and endosteal callus, often with a **linear band of sclerosis perpendicular to the long axis** of the bone (tibial shaft, metatarsals, femoral neck). The history (new running program, military recruit) is part of the diagnosis.

**Why you must not biopsy it.** Healing callus can mimic osteosarcoma histologically. MRI shows the fracture line and marrow edema and resolves the question.

**Avulsion cousins.** Old or healing avulsions at tendon attachments (ischial tuberosity, anterior inferior iliac spine, the posteromedial distal femur) can look aggressive, with irregular cortex and fluffy new bone. Location at a known attachment is the clue.

</div>

<div class="lesion">

### Normal variants that look like lesions

<div class="ex-slot" data-topic="variants"></div>

- **Cortical irregularity of the distal femur** ("cortical desmoid"): irregular, sometimes scalloped cortex at the posteromedial distal femoral metaphysis, at the attachment of the medial head of gastrocnemius and the adductor magnus, in adolescents. Leave it alone; it is a classic biopsy trap.
- **Humeral pseudocyst**: an apparent lucency in the greater tuberosity, where sparse trabeculae and overlying fat create a "lesion".
- **Dorsal defect of the patella**: a well-defined lucent defect in the superolateral patella in adolescents.
- **Calcaneal pseudocyst** (Ward triangle equivalent): an area of sparse trabeculae in the calcaneal neck.
- **Prominent nutrient canals and vascular channels**, and the normal **Ward triangle** of the femoral neck.

</div>

<div class="lesion">

### Others that are usually left alone

<div class="ex-slot" data-topic="others"></div>

- **Vertebral hemangioma**: coarse vertical trabeculae on radiographs ("corduroy"), "polka dots" on axial CT, and fat signal on T1 MRI. Fat-poor or expansile hemangiomas, or those with a soft-tissue component, are the exception.
- **Paget disease**: cortical thickening, coarse trabeculae and bone enlargement in an older adult. The lytic front in a long bone has a flame or "blade of grass" shape. Watch for new lytic destruction or a soft-tissue mass (rare sarcomatous change).
- **Subchondral cysts and geodes** in an arthritic joint, and **intraosseous ganglia** near a joint: lucent lesions on both sides of the joint with arthritis are degenerative.

</div>

## 1.5 The classic malignant bone tumors

A lesion you cannot name with confidence goes to Task 2. Score it, then think about who the patient is. Four primary malignant tumors are classically described on radiographs, and an R1 should recognize each one.

  <figcaption>Figure 1.5. The four classic malignant primary bone tumors. Schematic.</figcaption>
</figure>

<div class="lesion">

### Osteosarcoma

<div class="ex-slot" data-topic="osteosarcoma"></div>

**Who and where.** Adolescents and young adults, with a second peak in older adults (after Paget disease or radiation). The metaphysis around the knee (distal femur, proximal tibia) and the proximal humerus.

**What you see.** A mixed lytic and sclerotic lesion that **makes bone: cloud-like, fluffy osteoid matrix**, in the bone and in the soft-tissue mass. Aggressive periosteal reaction: **sunburst** spicules and a **Codman triangle**. Wide zone of transition.

**What the next step is.** MRI of the **entire bone** (tumor extent, joint and physis involvement, skip lesions) and chest CT for lung metastases, with referral to orthopedic oncology before biopsy.

</div>

<div class="lesion">

### Chondrosarcoma

<div class="ex-slot" data-topic="chondrosarcoma"></div>

**Who and where.** Adults, usually over 40. Pelvis, proximal femur, proximal humerus, ribs and shoulder girdle.

**What you see.** **Chondroid matrix (rings and arcs)** like an enchondroma, plus the features of aggression: **deep endosteal scalloping (more than two thirds of the cortex)**, cortical thickening or destruction, periosteal reaction, a soft-tissue mass, a large lesion, and **pain coming from the lesion** (Murphey et al. 1998). The radiologist's daily challenge is the line between enchondroma and low-grade chondrosarcoma (see Enchondroma above).

</div>

<div class="lesion">

### Ewing sarcoma

<div class="ex-slot" data-topic="ewing"></div>

**Who and where.** Children and young adults, roughly 5 to 25 years. The **diaphysis** or metadiaphysis of long bones, and the **flat bones** (pelvis, scapula, ribs), more so with increasing age.

**What you see.** **Permeative or moth-eaten** destruction, **lamellated ("onion skin")** or spiculated ("hair-on-end") periosteal reaction, and a **large soft-tissue mass** that is often bigger than the bone change suggests (Murphey et al. 2013). Fever and raised inflammatory markers can mimic osteomyelitis. In a flat bone the radiograph can look almost normal: see the [Ewing sarcoma of the scapula](/cases/#ewing-scapula).

</div>

<div class="lesion">

### Lymphoma of bone

<div class="ex-slot" data-topic="lymphoma"></div>

**Who and where.** Adults. Long bones (often the femur) and the pelvis.

**What you see.** Permeative or moth-eaten change that can be **subtle on radiographs**, with a **large soft-tissue mass while the cortex looks relatively preserved**: the tumor spreads through the small channels of the cortex rather than destroying it. MRI shows marrow replacement that is far more extensive than the radiograph suggests (Krishnan et al. 2003).

</div>

### Practical rules for Task 2

- **Age over 40, single lytic lesion**: metastasis and myeloma first, then primary bone tumors. A known primary adds 2 Bone-RADS points.
- **Permeative or moth-eaten destruction (IIIB)** in a child or young adult: Ewing sarcoma, lymphoma, osteomyelitis and Langerhans cell histiocytosis are the main differential. In an older adult: myeloma, lymphoma and metastasis.
- **Flat bones** (pelvis, scapula, ribs) are where aggressive lesions hide. Many lucent axial lesions are Bone-RADS 0 on radiographs: get CT or MRI.
- **Do not order the biopsy yourself, or let it be done anywhere, before the patient is seen by the team that will operate.** The biopsy tract may have to be removed with the tumor, and poorly planned biopsies of malignant bone tumors lead to changed treatment, including amputation that would otherwise not have been needed (Mankin et al. 1982). Your report should say: "Referral to orthopedic oncology is recommended before biopsy."

## 1.6 How to say it in the report

| Situation | Words that help the clinician |
| --- | --- |
| Do not touch | "Non-ossifying fibroma. Benign. No further imaging or follow-up is needed (Bone-RADS 1)." |
| Benign, but confirm | "Lucent geographic lesion without aggressive features, most likely enchondroma (Bone-RADS 2). Recommend radiographs in 3–6 months to confirm stability." |
| Not sure, can't see it | "Lucent lesion of the iliac wing, incompletely evaluated on radiographs (Bone-RADS 0). Recommend CT or MRI." |
| Aggressive | "Aggressive lytic lesion of the proximal tibia with permeative margins and lamellated periosteal reaction (Bone-RADS 4). Recommend MRI of the entire bone and urgent referral to orthopedic oncology before biopsy." |

Name the category, name the next step, and say how soon. A vague "clinical correlation recommended" is not a plan.

# Part B. Soft-tissue masses

## 1.7 The core concept: benign or malignant?

The question is the same as in bone: **is this a lesion I can name and leave, or could it be a sarcoma?** Most soft-tissue lumps are benign (lipomas and cysts above all), but a sarcoma treated as a "lump" and shelled out without imaging is a disaster for the patient. Your job is to spot the red flags.

| Feature | Usually benign | Red flag for malignancy |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Depth** | Superficial: in the subcutaneous fat, above the fascia | Deep: below the fascia, in or between muscles |
| **Size** | Under 5 cm | 5 cm or more, or growing |
| **Margin** | Well circumscribed | Infiltrative; crosses fascial planes |
| **Neighbors** | Respects fascia, muscle, vessels, nerves and bone | Invades muscle, encases the neurovascular bundle, touches or erodes bone |
| **Internal structure** | Homogeneous | Heterogeneous, with necrosis or hemorrhage |
| **Vascularity** | None or little | Internal vascularity on Doppler, solid enhancement on MRI |

  <img src="/book/figures/soft-tissue-compartments.svg" alt="Two schematic thigh cross-sections. Left: a small oval mass in the subcutaneous fat above the fascia. Right: a large heterogeneous deep mass inside the muscle that breaks through the fascia, contains necrosis and vessels, encases the neurovascular bundle and touches the bone." />
  <figcaption>Figure 1.6. Where the mass sits matters. Learn the layers: skin, subcutaneous fat, fascia, muscle, neurovascular bundle, bone.</figcaption>
</figure>

**These are red flags, not rules.** In a series of 266 extremity soft-tissue tumors with pathology, 43% of the benign tumors were larger than 5 cm and 57% were deep, and about a quarter of the sarcomas were superficial. On MRI, heterogeneous signal and size were stronger predictors than depth (Chung et al. 2012). So a small superficial mass is not automatically benign, and a deep lipoma is not automatically a sarcoma. The practical rule: **a mass you cannot confidently name as benign needs imaging, and an indeterminate mass is treated as a possible sarcoma until proven otherwise.** The ACR puts it simply: imaging should be performed in a patient with a suspected soft-tissue mass that cannot be clinically confirmed as benign (Garner et al. 2023).

## 1.8 What each modality adds

Ultrasound: cyst or solid, above or below the fascia, Doppler vascularity, compressibility, needle guidance. MRI: fat, fluid or blood, heterogeneity and necrosis, enhancement, relation to fascia, vessels, nerves and bone, staging." />
  <figcaption>Figure 1.7. Radiograph, ultrasound and MRI each answer a different question about a soft-tissue mass.</figcaption>

- **Radiograph: always worth a look.** It shows what ultrasound and MRI can miss: **phleboliths** (small round calcifications with a lucent center) in a vascular malformation; **ossification**, which must be mature at the periphery in myositis ossificans (Part A); a **fat lucency** in a lipoma; and **bone involvement**, from smooth pressure erosion (slow, usually benign) to destruction (aggressive).
- **Ultrasound: the first test for a superficial lump.** Is it a **cyst** (anechoic, posterior acoustic enhancement) or **solid**? Is it **above or below the fascia**? Does **Doppler** show internal vessels? Is it **compressible** (lipoma, vascular malformation) or does it **move with a tendon** (ganglion, giant cell tumor of tendon sheath)? A typical superficial lipoma is oval, parallel to the skin, and iso- or slightly hyperechoic to fat with thin echogenic lines.
- **MRI: the test for a deep, large or indeterminate mass.** T1 tells you whether the mass is **fat** (Chapter 2); fluid-sensitive images and contrast tell you **cyst versus solid tissue**, **necrosis** and **heterogeneity**; and MRI maps the mass against the **fascia, muscles, neurovascular bundle and bone** for the surgeon.

## 1.9 Soft-tissue masses you can name

Like the do-not-touch bone lesions, some soft-tissue masses can be named on imaging. They will each get their own section later in the book; for now, know their names and their single best clue.

| Mass | The clue |
| --- | --- |
| **Lipoma** | Follows fat on every sequence, with at most thin septa. Thick or nodular septa, or non-fatty nodules, raise atypical lipomatous tumor or liposarcoma. |
| **Ganglion and synovial cysts** | Anechoic or fluid signal, near a joint or tendon sheath, often with a neck to the joint. |
| **Vascular malformation** | Phleboliths on radiographs; lobulated fluid-bright channels with fat on MRI. |
| **Peripheral nerve sheath tumor** | Fusiform mass along a nerve, the nerve entering and leaving it, split-fat sign, target sign on T2. |
| **Giant cell tumor of tendon sheath** | Mass on a finger tendon sheath, low signal on T2 and blooming on gradient echo (hemosiderin). |
| **Elastofibroma** | Between the chest wall and the inferior tip of the scapula in an older adult, often both sides, fat streaks inside. |

## 1.10 How to say it in the report

| Situation | Words that help the clinician |
| --- | --- |
| Benign, named | "3 cm superficial lipoma in the subcutaneous fat of the posterior shoulder. No further imaging is needed." |
| Indeterminate | "6 cm heterogeneous solid mass deep to the fascia of the anterior thigh, abutting the femoral neurovascular bundle. A soft-tissue sarcoma cannot be excluded. Recommend referral to a sarcoma center before biopsy or excision." |

Say where it is (above or below the fascia), how big it is, whether it is solid or cystic, and what it touches. Never recommend "excision" for a mass you cannot name.

<div class="never">

### Never forget

**Bone**

- The margin is the lesion's speedometer. Look at it first.
- A lesion earns the benign bin only with positive, classic features. "Nothing scary" is not a diagnosis.
- Do not touch means do not biopsy, not do not help. A painful osteoid osteoma still gets treated.
- Myositis ossificans and healing stress fractures can fool the pathologist. Follow them, do not needle them.
- A cartilage lesion that hurts, scallops more than two thirds of the cortex, or breaks out is chondrosarcoma until proven otherwise.
- Over 40 with a single lytic lesion: metastasis and myeloma first.
- The biopsy belongs to the surgeon who will do the resection.

**Soft tissue**

- Know your layers: above the fascia or below it is the first question.
- Deep, 5 cm or more, growing, heterogeneous or vascular: think sarcoma.
- Red flags, not rules: benign masses can be deep and large, and sarcomas can be superficial.
- Get a radiograph. Phleboliths, ossification and bone involvement are easy to miss on MRI.
- A mass you cannot name is not "shelled out". It goes to the sarcoma team first.
