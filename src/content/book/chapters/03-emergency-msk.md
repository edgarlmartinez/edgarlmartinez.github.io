The night shift is where MSK radiology is most unforgiving: the patient goes home on your read. This chapter collects the injuries and infections that are **easy to miss and costly to miss**. It starts with the Lisfranc injury, because it is the classic example: common enough to see every month, subtle enough to be missed on the first radiograph, and disabling when it is.

# Trauma

## 3.1 The Lisfranc injury

A Lisfranc injury is any injury to the **tarsometatarsal (TMT) joint complex**, from a ligament sprain to a complete fracture-dislocation. It is the most common dislocation in the foot. The mechanism can be direct (a crush) or indirect, typically **forefoot abduction or an axial load on a plantarflexed foot** (missing a step, a bad landing, a foot caught under a falling rider).

### Anatomy you must know

- The TMT joint is where the **metatarsal bases meet the tarsus**. **M1, M2 and M3 articulate with the medial, intermediate and lateral cuneiforms (C1, C2, C3)**; **M4 and M5 articulate with the cuboid**.
- The TMT, intercuneiform, cuneocuboid and intermetatarsal joints and their ligaments form one **Lisfranc joint complex**, anatomically and mechanically.
- The **base of M2 is recessed** between C1 and C3, like a keystone. This is what stabilizes the whole midfoot.
- **There is no ligament between the bases of M1 and M2.** The link is the **Lisfranc ligament**, which runs obliquely from the **medial cuneiform (C1) to the base of M2**. It has **dorsal, interosseous and plantar** components. The **interosseous** component is the **thickest and strongest (5–7 mm)**, with superior and inferior bands, and its disruption makes the TMT joint unstable.
- Intermetatarsal, intercuneiform and C3–cuboid ligaments reinforce the front and back of the TMT articulation.

### How to read the radiographs

Get **AP, oblique and lateral** views, and check the same lines every time (Siddiqui et al. 2014):

1. **AP view: the medial edge of the M2 base lines up with the medial edge of C2.** Any step-off is abnormal.
2. **Oblique view: the medial edge of M3 lines up with the medial edge of C3**, and the medial edge of M4 lines up with the medial edge of the cuboid.
3. **Lateral view: no dorsal step-off** of the metatarsal bases relative to the tarsus.
4. **The C1–M2 interval**: widening between the medial cuneiform and the base of M2, best judged against the other foot or on a weight-bearing view. (The author's team is building an AI tool to measure this interval: see [AI projects](/ai/).)
5. **The fleck sign**: a small bone fragment in the C1–M2 space is the Lisfranc ligament avulsed from M2 or C1. It means the ligament is injured.

Radiographs can look normal or almost normal. **If the history fits** (midfoot pain, swelling, plantar bruising, inability to bear weight), **say so in the report and recommend weight-bearing radiographs, CT or MRI.** CT shows small fractures and the full fracture pattern for the surgeon; MRI shows the ligament itself.

### Classification: which way did the metatarsals go?

The simplest classification describes the direction in which the metatarsal bases displace relative to the tarsus:

- **Isolated**: one or two metatarsals displaced.
- **Homolateral**: all, or M2–M5, displaced laterally in the same direction.
- **Divergent**: M1 displaced medially while M2–M5 displace laterally.

These are the cases that are missed. The patient walks in, the radiograph "looks fine", and months later the midfoot collapses into arthritis.

### What the surgeon needs, and after surgery

- **Is the joint unstable?** Any displacement, widening of the C1–M2 interval, a fleck fragment, or a disrupted interosseous ligament on MRI suggests instability, which usually means fixation. A stable sprain can be treated in a boot.
- **Which columns are involved**, which fractures are intra-articular, and how comminuted the bases are (CT).
- **After fixation**, check alignment of the TMT joints, the position of the screws or plates, and later, healing or arthrodesis. Complications include post-traumatic arthritis, deformity and chronic pain.

### Never forget (Lisfranc)

- On every AP foot: the medial edge of M2 lines up with the medial edge of C2.
- There is no ligament between M1 and M2. The Lisfranc ligament runs from C1 to M2.
- A fleck in the C1–M2 interval is a ligament injury until proven otherwise.
- A "normal" radiograph does not exclude a Lisfranc injury. If the story fits, ask for weight-bearing views, CT or MRI.
- Name the pattern (isolated, homolateral, divergent) and say whether the joint looks unstable.
